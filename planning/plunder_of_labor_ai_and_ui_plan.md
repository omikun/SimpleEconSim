# Plunder of Labor: profit driven AI and visible consequences

## Goal and scope

**Strategic direction, updated 2026-10-10:** The player trades expansion in the exploitation of labor and territory against citizens' livelihoods, while neighboring nations compete for markets, resources, and military advantage. Domestic production and private profit matter strategically through actual investment, tax receipts, supplies, and funded forces. A country can protect its citizens yet lose leverage abroad; it can expand and grow richer while impoverishing people or becoming unable to sustain its commitments. This tension defines the first playable slice in [Labor, Territory, and Rivalry](plunder_of_labor_first_slice.md).

Give firms, landlords, creditors, households, and governments distinct decisions. A household with a workable livelihood should usually prefer to keep it unless a job offers a better *reliable* life. An employer wants the lowest **feasible total cost per saleable unit**, which can mean better pay, a machine, a lease, a political petition, or contraction. When profits are squeezed, owners may try to change households' alternatives or their bargaining power. They must pay for the attempt, can fail, and cannot enact law themselves. The player should be able to follow a decision from motive through target, legal stage, household response, and realized outcome.

This extends [the mechanism design](plunder_of_labor.md) and [the implementation playbook](plunder_of_labor_implementation_playbook.md). The existing playbook calls the implemented work a **first causal slice**. Do not assume every mechanism in the design catalog has an executable operation. In particular, a narrative event must never claim a destroyed farm, pass arrest, document seizure, or restored right unless the simulation actually changed the named asset, case, or right.

## Gameplay review: what the first plan missed

**Review, 2026-10-09:** The first version specified a useful simulation and inspection system, but it did not yet specify a satisfying game. Its main risk was that increasingly capable owners would act while the player watched increasingly detailed reports. More mechanisms and smarter optimization alone do not solve that. This revision makes player decisions and a playable experiment prerequisites for broad AI expansion.

| Gap in the first plan | Why it matters for play | Design correction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unclear player role and desired outcome | The player could inspect a crisis without knowing what they are trying to achieve or control. | Start with the player governing a coalition. Give the scenario a public mandate, a horizon, and distinct outcome measures. |
| Responses deferred while coercion expands | Enact/repeal alone turns the game into repeatedly vetoing owners or helplessly watching them. | Deliver a small set of executable protective, productive, negotiated, and coercive responses with the first AI pathway. |
| Greedy choices over 2–4 turns | An owner cannot deliberately build a land, credit, and political strategy whose costs precede its return. | Add persistent strategic goals, staged projects, and forecasts that reach the relevant harvest/repayment cycle. |
| Everyone optimizes independently | Identical owners spam the same law; rivals never poach labor or dispute who receives the benefit. | Model rival bids, contested targets, distinct interests, and shared benefits from lobbying. |
| Household agency mainly means accepting/refusing | People appear as passive inputs whose only political expression is a rising unrest number. | Give existing worker/community organizations concrete demands, offers, and responses. Add missing negotiation operations explicitly. |
| All costs appear in one owner calculation | If the owner automatically pays every public or social cost, the model erases the incentive to shift costs onto others. | Keep owner, state, household, and social accounts distinct. Charge the owner only for costs it actually bears or expects to bear. |
| Inspection presented as the main UI | A truthful dashboard can still overwhelm the player and offer little anticipation or control. | Put an actionable situation, a deadline, and a few alternatives first; put evidence behind inspection. |
| No definition of pacing, recovery, or progress | Repeated crises become exhausting; one early error can make the remaining turns pointless. | Telegraph threats, allow recovery and durable settlements, and recognize improvements that persist. |
| Technical tests stand in for enjoyment | Conservation and diverse outcomes do not establish that choices are understandable or interesting. | Test a short playable loop with people before implementing the full catalog. |
| External competition was too weak | A secure domestic bargain could remove the main strategic pressure without confronting neighboring powers. | Put an economically and militarily active rival, shared customers, and inhabited border territory in the first slice. |

### High level goal: remain competitive without destroying the society that sustains you

The central player question is: **How much labor and territory will you mobilize to remain economically and militarily competitive, who gains from that expansion, and what livelihoods are you willing to put at risk?** Food security, production, treasury, popular support, and freedom to refuse work interact with competitors' investment, export prices, force readiness, and territorial ambitions. Relative power matters alongside absolute welfare. Rival nations have their own citizens, budgets, and constraints, and can also overextend.

For the first prototype, the player governs one of two neighboring nations through a 20-turn cotton, livelihood, and border competition scenario. Each has an industrial capital and an inhabited agricultural border district. They compete for funded cloth sales and can bargain over, defend, or seize the other's border district. Use a bounded variant of the existing Empire of Cotton scaffolding, with its own scenario ID and conserved engine operations. The mandate is to maintain a functioning, sovereign state while choosing the extent and human cost of its economic and territorial ambitions. Briefing and debrief make the rival's relative gains, local losses, and future commitments visible. See the first-slice document for exact boundaries and implementation order.

The debrief should report relative export earnings and sustainable military strength, territory and sovereignty, household security (including the worst affected group and people in acquired territory), productive capacity and solvency, distribution of land/debt/income, and effective freedom to refuse or leave work. Report unresolved arrears, depleted food, outstanding projects, and expected upkeep at the horizon so the last turn cannot reward borrowing or exhausting everything without consequence. Territorial gain is never a complete measure of success. Regime change can continue play under a changed coalition when the scenario supports it; do not assume it always ends the game.

An attractive outcome can be a durable bargain: reliable wages, functioning mills, protected commons, and enough productive and military capacity or diplomacy to maintain that settlement among rivals. Other runs can end in an expanding but fragile empire, a prosperous yet dependent state, citizen impoverishment with military superiority, lost territory, or viable restrained development. Some policies may improve both livelihoods and output. Do not add an arbitrary penalty whenever the player does something beneficial merely to manufacture a dilemma. Constraints should arise from real cash, goods, time, land, authority, and competing commitments. The rival responds to interests and opportunity, not to a hidden rule that punishes humane policy.

Some passages in the broader GDD describe dispossession or technological immiseration as inevitable. For this feature, implement those as possible causal pathways whose conditions can be changed. Otherwise the simulation predetermines the answer and makes the player's strategy cosmetic. Preserve the subject and its consequences while allowing the rules to demonstrate when exploitation pays its beneficiaries, when it fails, and when a different arrangement succeeds. Likewise, count partial livelihoods and effective rights; a larger wage sector does not by itself prove dispossession, and a smaller one does not prove household security.

For example, a player pursuing a **productive compromise** might preserve harvest access, use grain reserves to bridge a shortage, and negotiate later seasonal mill work while the owner repairs machinery. A player pursuing an **export coalition** might back an owner's land claim to secure export receipts, then face the actual food, treasury, and political consequences of enforcing it. A player pursuing **local autonomy** might protect land and reduce dependence on the mill, accepting lower export receipts while seeking a funded alternative source of public revenue. These are illustrative strategies, not scripted branches or guaranteed balanced packages: each needs a feasible sequence of operations, and the same sequence can succeed in one market and fail in another.

### The repeatable player loop

1. **Read the situation:** one concrete threat or opportunity, its cause, affected people, and the next decision window. Example: a mill wants a gathering restriction before its next payroll; villagers offer seasonal labor if harvest access is protected.
2. **Form a response:** compare a few feasible choices with immediate cost, delayed effect, likely supporters/opponents, uncertainty, and the cost of waiting. Inspection is free. A material intervention consumes the scenario's existing major-decision budget and any real resources it requires.
3. **Commit and watch:** an actor makes a credible response, contracts or rights change, and output/food/pay evolve. The player should normally see a first consequence within 1–3 turns while longer projects remain visible.
4. **Adapt or consolidate:** renegotiate, fund implementation, change course, or let a working settlement run. Routine collection and payroll should be delegated to standing policies; repeated clicking is not the challenge.

Every round also asks where to allocate scarce capacity: household relief, productive investment, or defense/expansion. Export losses can constrain future public income; soldiers draw on people and food otherwise available to farms and firms. Higher private profit supports the state only through actual fiscal transfers. New territory can provide resources and tax receipts after it is secured, while adding residents, supply distances, opposition, and garrison costs. Diplomacy and trade can reduce these pressures when both nations find an agreement worthwhile.

Use the existing Guided composition described in [the GDD](../gdd.md): objective, up to three priority pressures, contextual actions, and an Analysis drawer. Reveal detail as it becomes useful. Reserve pauses for an actionable deadline or an irreversible escalation; batch routine reports. Warnings need a genuine response window, subject to plausible notice. An information system can curate existing events but must not invent a famine, erase a successful policy, or secretly strengthen an opponent to keep the game dramatic.

### Player authority and meaningful counterplay

In player-controlled territory, AI ministers may recommend a petition decision or follow an explicitly delegated standing policy. They must not enact a law and overrule the player between turns without an existing constitutional or political mechanism. Independent government AI governs other jurisdictions. Both use the same legal and resource constraints. A command in Advanced view must consume the same resources and decision budget as its Guided equivalent.

| Player response | Concrete effect required | Cost, obstacle, or opposing interest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Refuse or condition an owner petition | Stop the proposed law, or accept enforceable terms such as access exemptions or a funded pay agreement. | The firm can reduce output, change investment, negotiate, or withdraw if that is economically feasible. Refusal does not generate a scripted punishment. |
| Protect or restore subsistence access | Preserve a named right, or fund/authorize actual restoration after a loss. | Adjudication, contested possession, and implementation capacity where applicable; repeal alone may leave physical access unchanged. |
| Negotiate a labor settlement | Worker/owner acceptance of funded wages, hours, or seasonal availability; record breaches and remedies. | More payroll or less available work time; better retention or production may compensate. |
| Bridge a temporary crisis | Deliver existing grain or a funded cash transfer to named recipients; audit the transfer. | Finite reserves and another use of the treasury; relief can improve a household's ability to reject poor terms. |
| Improve productive capacity | Repair machinery, fund an available project, or change a real supply/input arrangement. | Cash, materials, lead time, demand risk, and who owns the resulting asset. |
| Enact and fund owner-backed pressure | Use the actual law, petition, and case process. | Household loss, political opposition, state expense, and uncertain economic return; show the distribution of costs. |
| Defend, expand, or negotiate abroad | Recruit actual people, pay/feed and move units, resolve a contest for an inhabited district, or enter an enforceable treaty. | Foregone civilian work, recurrent supply, casualties, diplomatic risk, and administration of acquired territory. |

The first playable slice needs at least one actual protective response, one productive/negotiated response, and the existing coercive proposal. If a required operation is missing, implementing it is part of this feature. Do not defer all counterplay while giving owners the full catalog. Later add debt restructuring, collective credit/ownership, and alternative buyers only with complete operations and recipients. Optional district policies and standing rules should reduce micromanagement as the world grows.

### Strategy, resistance, and the market around the actors

Owners need persistent goals such as securing next harvest's labor, surviving a debt deadline, expanding a profitable sector, acquiring land, or retaining a buyer. Each goal has a budget, intended beneficiaries, milestones, expiry/review conditions, and an exit option. A plan may start with an advance or petition whose immediate return is negative because later steps could justify it. Each step can be refused, pre-empted, or invalidated; follow-up actions are never guaranteed. This is a bounded hierarchy of goal → project → action, rather than exhaustive world search.

Give owners persistent differences drawn from existing traits and circumstances: liquidity, patience, risk tolerance, political connections, specialization, and past success. A leveraged mill and a land-rich landlord should often want different policies. Firms compete for workers and buyers; a rival's better offer can defeat a low wage strategy. A law may benefit nonsponsors too, so do not award each owner all regional wage savings. Start with these shared-benefit and rival-bid effects; cartel or coalition bargaining can be a later explicit operation.

Worker organizations and communities should pursue concrete ends such as retaining a plot, recovering unpaid wages, protecting harvest time, or securing lawful exit. Build on existing factions and resistance, but connect a demand, representative, offered settlement, acceptance, and subsequent action. Collective action requires participation, time, and resources; hunger alone is not guaranteed successful organization. Show internal differences between tenants, landless workers, skilled workers, and cultivators instead of treating “labor” as one interest. Household protection and successful negotiation can persist, even when owners dislike the outcome.

Spatial and seasonal constraints should affect strategies where the engine models them: reaching another employer, the value of harvest labor, a creditor's control of the local buyer, and the difference between an export market and local food demand. Lower wages can reduce local demand; cheap labor cannot repair missing inputs or a nonexistent buyer. External buyers must have funded purchases or an explicit modeled trade boundary. Required returns, investment decisions, and price expectations should reflect existing debt and observed opportunities; keep provisional tuning values visible and avoid giving every firm the same eternal margin target.

Nation AI needs its own project above the owner layer: preserve market position, fund modernization, secure a valuable border district, deter a rival, or negotiate access. It uses observed delivered costs and receipts, treasury/runway, deployable supplied strength, and domestic constraints. It can exploit a player's weakness without sharing the owners' objective of cheapest labor. Both levels use the same real budgets and capacities; no automatic conversion from corporate profit to national military strength. Separate private lobbying claims about “national competitiveness” from the measured public benefit of a proposal.

### Information, causality, and fair difficulty

AI observes prices, its own books, recent hiring outcomes, public law, and information it could plausibly obtain. It does not read another household's perfect future utility or the next harvest shock. Forecasts have a source, age, and uncertainty. Fixed seeds should reproduce a run, but stable ID ordering must not always give the same firm first access to every worker, plot, or officer; use a deterministic rotating/seeded resolution order and recheck shared budgets and targets when committing.

Default Guided play can explain public behavior and give a readable estimate of consequences. Exact private AI scores and every rejected candidate belong in Analysis/debug unless a scenario explicitly grants that knowledge. Deeper espionage is unnecessary for this slice. Difficulty should change starting constraints, information quality, actor competence, or negotiation room while preserving the same economy and legal rules; do not make hidden free cash or unavoidable coercion the challenge.

An event link proves a direct transfer or state change, not that it caused every later change in profit or hunger. The debrief must separate direct effects, observed subsequent changes, and estimated contribution. Compare matched seeds/treatments in development to test attribution; in the player's timeline use “followed by” or “estimated effect” when other changes could explain the result. No exact invented counterfactual profit.

### Small playable experiment and evidence of fun

Build the [two-nation first slice](plunder_of_labor_first_slice.md): one domestic labor conflict linked to actual export competition and a contestable inhabited border. Use one pressure mechanism (a targeted gathering restriction), better voluntary offers, funded relief/access protection, one productive investment, and a small supplied military. Include a diplomatic way to stabilize the border. Add controllable harvest/price conditions in the test harness to exercise adaptation; production play follows the scenario's declared market/weather rules. This replaces the earlier isolated-district prototype.

Evaluate provisional targets with a small formative playtest, ideally 3–5 people unfamiliar with the internals. These are design hypotheses, not proof of enjoyment or statistical balance:

- After the opening, a player can explain whom they govern, what is at risk, and name two feasible responses without opening an accounting table.
- They can explain how a domestic labor or spending choice changed relative market position or border security, and what the rival actually spent to obtain its advantage.
- Before an important choice, they can describe the expected benefit, who bears its cost, and one uncertainty. After the result, they can explain why an actor accepted, refused, or changed strategy.
- At least two approaches are viable in the prototype under suitable conditions. A strategy that wins everywhere, doing nothing that dominates every active choice, or mandatory coercion is a design finding to investigate, not a target to force.
- The player sees a durable improvement, a meaningful setback, and a recovery opportunity where those arise from the run. Track repetitive vetoes, inexplicable events, unavoidable cascades, time spent searching for a control, and turns spent only dismissing alerts.
- At the end, ask which decision was interesting, which felt arbitrary, and what they would try differently. If they understand the economics but do not want another run, revisit the decisions and pacing before adding mechanisms.

Do not use an automated test to declare the game fun. Tests establish rules and prevent exploits; observation establishes whether people can use those rules to make interesting plans.

## Decision model

### Household outside option

For each real person, forecast immediate food/cash needs and the next relevant harvest or obligation using current **usable** plot/commons yield, stored food, crop sale proceeds, craft/informal work where executable, tenancy terms, cash due, food prices, care/rest time where represented, and the chance of a harvest or wage payment. Compare this with funded offers' expected *cash equivalent* after deductions, hours, food/transport cost, injury/quit risk, and payment reliability. Count alternative and mixed uses of time only when production supports them; do not give an employed worker a full day's foraging too. Secure subsistence raises the value of declining a poor offer; it does not ban high wage work. Hunger or debt changes the feasible options and urgency, not the person's innate willingness to be deceived. Literacy can affect contract information or dispute success only through an explicit rule with a remedy.

Use `labor_choice.evaluate_offer` for final employment acceptance. Extend its input/decision record rather than creating another hiring gate. For tenancy/advance, add separate proposal and acceptance results before calling the existing creation/transfer APIs; do not silently enroll a tenant or borrower. Only include migration, relief, trade, or resistance as candidate choices where a real simulation operation is reachable. If no funded job exists, pressure can produce unemployment, tenancy, flight, protest, or hunger; it cannot create an employee.

### Owner pressure, alternatives, and objective

At the end of each settled turn, keep a rolling 3–5 turn record per company: sold revenue, output, unsold stock, actual wage cash/scrip liability, inputs, rent, tax, interest, repairs, petitions, advances and recoveries, guards/case contributions, and cash runway in payrolls. A firm is under pressure if its rolling operating margin is below its required return, cash runway is short, or funded vacancies constrain profitable output. Falling commodity prices and unsold inventory are distinct from labor scarcity. Compare price against **probable sales**, not production alone.

At the next owner planning window, review the actor's persistent goal and enumerate feasible projects/actions plus a no-action baseline. Use a bounded 2–4 turn operational forecast and a coarse project forecast reaching the relevant harvest, repayment, or capital payback period. Include residual asset/liability value at the boundary so long investments are not automatically rejected. For candidate project `a`, estimate:

`Δ owner value(a) = discounted[Δ expected sales receipts + Δ rents/credit receipts − Δ wages − Δ inputs/capital − Δ petition/survey/enforcement − Δ defaults/turnover/disruption] − failure risk − liquidity cost`.

Use company cash profit for a company's payroll strategy; use the landlord's rent/land value and the creditor's actual recovery for their choices. Charge this calculation only for the actor's expected share of expense: a treasury-funded patrol is a public cost until a tax, fee, or other actual liability shifts it to the firm. Record household and ecological harm separately; it enters owner value when it changes demand, labor supply, fines, resistance, or another modeled consequence. Avoid subtracting a bad outcome both inside probability-weighted cash flows and again as the same risk penalty. When the same owner controls two entities, an internal transfer is not new wealth. Use net output per hour and likely fill rate: the smallest posted wage may cost more through vacancies, fatigue, illness, quality loss, and strike. Report assumptions as forecasts and later compare them with actual results; do not call a forecast an observed saving.

Candidate families, all run through existing legal/action entry points:

1. **Market response:** raise or lower the wage within law and worker acceptance, improve terms, change staffing or shift/pace, maintain or buy machinery, import inputs/recruit through an actual route, sell stock, contract, close, or wait. If there is no saleable demand, contraction may win.
2. **Control the independent option:** as a qualified lord/holder, seek enclosure of a named plot, use an existing rent claim, or offer a lease/share agreement. Count lost food production, survey cost, enforcement chance, and worker migration. A firm with no land rights cannot perform this itself.
3. **Creditor path:** make a funded harvest advance to an eligible cultivator on a real plot, possibly with a crop lien. Price repayment and default, not face value; a loan disbursement is a cash outflow. A borrower can decline, repay, dispute, or default under the implemented rules.
4. **Political path:** pay to petition for wage cap, gathering restriction, quit penalty, or vagrancy; wait for an independent state decision. Value the chance of enactment **and** the chance of funded enforcement. A petition by itself changes neither law nor physical access.
5. **Legally gated force/captive regimes:** only expose an existing transformation operator when its historical/scenario/authority and target preconditions hold. Estimate military/upkeep, mortality, resistance, output disruption, and diplomatic/political cost. New mechanisms from the design catalog require their own engine ticket before the planner may select them.

For each candidate, record `feasible`, a specific rejection reason, expected incremental value, target IDs, cash required now, expected horizon, and main uncertainty. Pick the best positive risk-adjusted project value, using persistent actor preferences and deterministic tie breaking. An individual step can have a negative immediate return if its parent project's expected return remains positive; later milestones must be re-evaluated. Execute at most one **major** institutional/land action per owner per planning interval. Ordinary production and payroll continue. Add cooldowns for duplicate attempts, milestone deadlines, and hysteresis to prevent meaningless strategy oscillation; material news can justify a review. Shared courts, officers, and treasury impose regional limits too. If every project is negative, wait or contract. A profitable firm may still prefer an opportunistic action if its expected gain is positive, but a low margin alone is never a license to use force.

### Government is a separate actor

The state receives petitions and can originate tax, enforcement funding, or reform policy. Independent AI governments rank public revenue, governing coalition support, legitimacy, food security/unrest, court capacity, and treasury cost according to their regime/traits. In the player's jurisdiction this becomes a recommendation or an explicitly delegated rule, respecting the player's decision window and authority. A fee may influence access or corruption only through a recorded rule; it must not mechanically guarantee approval. The state may reject a rich sponsor, pass a law that later lacks officers, fund a particular case, or repeal/reform in response to harm. A player command uses the same validation and event schema as an AI decision, including jurisdiction, resource, and scenario action-budget checks.

Keep these stages separate: `proposed → decided → in force on paper → case funded → case resolved → physical access/worker state changed`. Rejection, delay, appeal, weak enforcement, and de facto access are valid outcomes. Tying owner pressure directly to `set_labor_law` or direct arrest would bypass the simulation's political conflict.

### Order, observation, and feedback

Audit the beginning-of-turn law decision, rent/tax assessment, labor matching, work, sales, and settlement order in `micro/region/stepper.py`. Read **last turn's sealed** financial record when planning; never use current unsettled production as realized profit. Resolve the current mismatch in which ordinary hiring precedes the elite planner but the mobility pass follows it: snapshot available information, publish this turn's offers/decisions, then match through one acceptance gate. If changing the order requires staging new offers for the next turn, make that rule consistent for both paths and show it in the UI. Revalidate funds, capacity, and competing claims at commit time. Petitions retain a visible decision window, and debt/liens settle at their normal due/harvest stage. Observe realized effects over the project's actual horizon and update bounded local success estimates (fill rate, petition acceptance, lien recovery, enforcement success), marking confounding changes. Do not use an opaque global reinforcement learner. Evaluation can be deterministic and cheap at tile scale.

## Data contract and UI narrative

Use stable IDs and plain JSON projections of authoritative simulation records, not a second mutable economy. Proposed records:

| Record | Minimum fields |
| --- | --- |
| `FirmOperatingStatement` | turn, firm/owner/tile IDs, sold revenue, units sold, produced/unsold units, wages paid and owed, inputs, rent/tax/interest, strategy/enforcement expense, cash profit, margin, runway; link to social cost audit separately |
| `LaborStrategyDecision` | ID, turn, actor role/ID, target firm/plot/person/case IDs, pressure trigger, baseline and chosen forecast, runner-up, rejected alternatives with reasons, legal preconditions, cash budget, expected effect turn, status |
| `HouseholdLivelihoodDecision` | person ID/turn, feasible real paths, usable food estimate, cash due, offer/net pay, reservation/outside estimate, chosen path and reason, linked strategy/rights/claim IDs |
| `GovernmentLawDecision` | petition/law/sponsor IDs, turn, coalition/legitimacy/treasury/capacity inputs, decision and reason, enforcement funding status |
| `MechanismOutcome` | source decision ID, turn, actual state change and transfers, target IDs, appeal/resistance result, observed profit/worker welfare delta; `pending` if too early to measure |
| `ActorStrategy` | actor/goal IDs, target and beneficiaries, project budget, milestones, next review, commitments, observable warning, cancellation/exit condition, outcome |
| `PlayerSituation` | source event IDs, objective at risk/opportunity, target, deadline, feasible actions with costs/authority, likely actor response, uncertainty, delegation status |

The last two records hold plans and presentation, never independent balances or invented events. Optional appeal, care, organization, and negotiation fields appear only after the corresponding operation exists.

Use a bounded recent event log per tile plus stable references to existing obligation, case, access, and petition IDs. Save/load must preserve pending decisions and outcome windows. The UI reads one server view model so labels and statuses agree across web and desktop. Avoid N×M household-by-candidate simulation: aggregate forecasts for ranking, then a bounded named target set for the selected action. Return a selection count and an explicit `sampled` label when the UI lists only part of a population.

### Player view, with concrete examples

The following are **Analysis and drilldown views**. The default Guided screen starts with the objective and up to three `PlayerSituation` cards. Each answers “what is happening, when must I act, and what can I change?” with a small number of feasible responses. A notification about an impending petition is clearly a proposal; the outcome ticker only reports completed events. Pin a few households/actors for continuity and let the player compare them with the regional distribution. Use text/icons as well as color, keyboard navigation, readable small-screen layouts, and “next important event” only when it will stop before a real response deadline.

1. **Tile labor pressure card** (web Citizens → Labor & Alienation; desktop selected tile): independent/tenant/wage/compulsory counts; funded vacancies; median offer and reservation; households with cash due; legal versus usable commons access; a small trend for wage hours, food access, firm cash margin, and enforcement spend. A compact status could read: `Cotton price fell 18%; 2 firms below required margin; 6 funded vacancies; 11 households retain usable commons`.
2. **Owner decision card** under each firm/estate: `Mill 17: cash margin −4% over 3 turns; 9/15 jobs filled. Seeks a wage cap; petition fee $8. Awaiting government decision.` In a scenario's authorized Analysis/debug view, add the owner's forecast and alternatives, for example `estimated +$12 over 3 turns; higher wage estimate +$9; machine +$5`. Show the **cash cost now**, project milestones, forecast uncertainty, subsequent measured return, and an affordance to inspect the affected owner/plot. The card exposes the actor's motive without implying its forecast is the truth.
3. **Law and case pipeline:** proposed/rejected/enacted labels, sponsor, turn, fee, government's stated reason, number of funded officers, target case, appeal, outcome.
